Quantum Information and Programming 101

From vectors to qubits to algorithms.
Predict first, then observe.

A course tool for a first course in quantum information and quantum programming. You work through each topic by committing to an answer, watching what the physics does, and explaining the difference. The same circuits then become code that runs on a simulator or on a real quantum computer.

How each question works

  1. Predict

    You commit to an answer before anything moves: a point on the sphere, a number, or a choice.

  2. Observe

    The tool shows what happens and tells you how your answer relates to it.

  3. Explain

    You write one sentence about the result, then compare it with a reference.

Committing first is what makes the observation informative. You find out exactly where your picture of the physics and the physics part ways.

    Pauli operators and Pauli strings in Qiskit
        
    Qiskit numbers the qubits from 0 and writes qubit 0 as the rightmost letter of a Pauli string. This course writes the first qubit first. So X⊗Z in this course, with X on the first qubit and Z on the second, is Pauli("ZX") in Qiskit. Mixing up the two orders gives wrong numbers and no error message.

    Research
    
    
    Research starts where Unit 4 ends: with a circuit on a real device and a result that needs explaining. The topic cards are in preparation.
    
    
    What a finished project looks like
    
    
    A finished project answers its question with evidence that someone else can check: the code, the raw counts, a control experiment, and a statement of what the result shows and what it does not show. A project that reaches this standard on a question that is still open can be written up for publication. That step happens outside this tool.

    Three experiments to start from
    
    
    The physics is that of Unit 1. The device techniques they need are the subject of Unit 4. Each one ends in a number with an uncertainty and a comparison with a model.
    
    
      
      
    • Readout calibration. Prepare |0⟩ and |1⟩, measure, and count how often the device reports the wrong one. Use the two error rates to correct a later measurement.
      • 
      
    • T1 and T2 against the model. Prepare |1⟩ or |+⟩, wait, and measure. Fit the decay and compare it with the noise model of this unit. Find where the model stops describing the data.
      • 
      
    • One circuit, three ways. Run the same small circuit on a device, on an ideal simulator, and on a simulator with a noise model. Apply one method of error mitigation and report how much of the gap it closes.

    About this tool
    
    
    Quantum Information and Programming 101 is a course tool for a first course in quantum information and quantum programming, by Linghua Zhu. It runs in the browser. There is no login, and answers are stored only on the device in use.
    
    
    How it is designed
    
    
    
      
    Predict first
    
      
    Every practice item asks for a commitment before anything moves: a point on the sphere, a number or a choice. Only then does the tool show what the physics does. Students who predict the outcome of a demonstration before seeing it understand it better than students who only watch (Crouch et al. 2004). Predict, observe and explain are modelled on the strategy elicit, confront, resolve (McDermott 2001).
    
      
    Feedback on the answer given
    
      
    The practice recognises common wrong answers. The feedback says what a wrong answer corresponds to, for example the state after the first gate alone, or the probability of the other outcome. It is about the task and the reasoning, not about the student. Feedback aimed at the person, such as praise, rarely helps learning (Hattie and Timperley 2007). The physics is computed in the page, so each prediction gets feedback specific to it, and the same answer always gets the same feedback.
    
      
    Help that fades
    
      
    Start here works through one example for each topic. In the practice the help then fades. The first items show the rotation axis, the sense of the turn or the measurement axis on the sphere. Later items show less, and the last ones show nothing. This adapts the fading of worked examples, which moves learners step by step from studying examples to solving problems on their own (Renkl and Atkinson 2003). After the starter set, further items come without hints. For gates alone there are .
    
      
    Missed items come back
    
      
    A missed item returns a few items later and again the next day. An item answered correctly returns after 3, 7, 14 and 30 days, without hints. Recalling an answer improves long-term retention more than studying it again (Roediger and Karpicke 2006). So does spreading practice over several days instead of one session (Cepeda et al. 2006).
    
      
    A reason for every option
    
      
    Each idea in a unit has one question for discussion. Students answer alone, talk it over and answer again (Crouch and Mazur 2001). Then they read the reasons for every option: why each wrong option is tempting, and where it breaks down. Several of the wrong options are answers that students give in published studies, listed in the tables below.
    
      
    One idea, several representations
    
      
    A state appears as amplitudes, as a point on the sphere, as numbers and as a circuit, and the worked examples translate from one to another (Ainsworth 2006). The Code part turns the circuit into a Qiskit program for a simulator or a real quantum computer. Students predict what the program prints, run it, and then change it. These are the first steps of the PRIMM approach to teaching programming (Sentance et al. 2019).
    
      
    Open to every student
    
      
    The tool is free and needs no account and no installation. It works on a phone as well as a computer, in a light and a dark theme, and with a keyboard alone. Every result is stated in words as well as drawn.
